A B S T R A C T
Human–robot collaborative polishing can integrate the capabilities of humans and automation to deal with
complex polishing tasks. Traditional impedance-control-based human–robot collaboration (HRC) requires
operators to physically interact with robots for a good polishing performance, which brings unsafety to
operators. To address this issue, a corrective shared control architecture using haptic feedback is proposed
in this paper, where the direct force-reflection is used to guarantee the exact human-intention intervention.
The proposed control architecture is designed with two layers: (i) the transparency layer in which the direct
force-reflection and the human–robot collaborative polishing strategy are implemented; (ii) the passivity layer
in which two energy tanks are designed and endowed with master and slave sides and a coupling energy
scaling policy is employed to guarantee the passivity of the whole system. Under the proposed architecture, the
constant force is adopted to polish normal areas of workpieces, and corrective force based on human intention
is applied to deal with unexpected issues. Finally, two groups of experiments are conducted to evaluate the
proposed architecture from two aspects: polishing effect and user experience.
1. Introduction
After rough machining, workpieces are usually left with residual
materials such as burrs on their surfaces, which affects the aesthetics
and even the service life of workpieces. Polishing is the last procedure
for workpiece machining and directly determines the quality of ma-
chined workpieces [1]. For complex workpieces, such as castings with
irregular shape and roughness [ 2] and weak-stiffness workpieces with
the deformation and polishing force fluctuation [ 3], the autonomous
polishing technology cannot handle these unexpected issues. Therefore,
some companies hire professional workers to polish these workpieces
manually. However, the manual mode has several drawbacks: low
efficiency, hazardous operation, and intensive labor [ 4]. In practice,
autonomous technologies can polish workpieces efficiently but cannot
handle unexpected issues, while manual mode can handle unexpected
issues but is inefficient. Therefore, combining human judgment and
decision-making capabilities with autonomous machining technology
is a promising solution in this field.
Human–robot collaboration (HRC) integrates the capabilities of hu-
mans and robots to finish the task. HRC can be generally categorized as
impedance-control-based HRC [ 5] and shared-control-based HRC [ 6].
Impedance-based HRC indicates that humans interact physically with
robots to affect robot behaviors directly. For example, Gaz et al. [ 7]

proposed a method based on the impedance-based HRC in polishing
tasks, where the robot held the polished workpiece and the operator
manually polished the workpiece while orienting it. One of the issues
with impedance-based HRC is operators’ safety in polishing tasks due to
the physical interaction between the operator and workpieces. Shared-
control-based HRC avoids the issue and provides comfort and security
to the operator because operators correct robot behaviors with teleoper-
ation interfaces [8]. In shared-control-based HRC, operators and robots
can work in two different locations thousands of meters apart [ 9].
Shared-control-based HRC includes three types [10]: (i) complemen-
tary shared control [11], the operator and local autonomous controller
are responsible for different and complementary subtasks (the operator
acts as a collaborator); (ii) blending shared control [ 12], the operator
inputs and nominal behavior are blended as the final input; that is,
the operator can intervene the nominal robot behavior (as a super-
visor); and (iii) assistive shared control, the operator takes the lead
in tasks with some assistive methods (as a leader), such as virtual
fixture [13,14] and prior knowledge method [15]. The complementary
shared control has a fixed level of automation (the level of automation
means the degree of the functions conducted autonomously) since
subtasks are assigned in advance. For example, González et al. [ 8]
proposed a complementary shared control system for polishing tasks,

where the operator controls the position of the polishing tool and the
local autonomous controller is responsible for the perpendicular and
smooth approach of the polishing tool to the workpiece surface; that
is, the operator and local autonomous controller are in a cooperative
relationship by utilizing their strengths. The blending shared control
has a more flexible level of automation, in which the operator is a
supervisor and will intervene to correct behaviors if necessary. The
assistive shared control is suitable for complex tasks requiring real-
time decision-making by the operator or tasks with complicated steps.
Therefore, the blending shared control is more suitable for polishing
tasks because a constant force is necessary to act on the normal area of
workpieces, and an operator’s judgment and intervention are required
when handling abnormalities.
For the blending shared control, the degree of integration of hu-
man and automation capabilities (degree of corrective intervention)
is mainly determined by human intention estimation [16]. Hagenow
et al. [17] proposed corrective shared autonomy, where the nominal
robot behavior was generated by dynamic movement primitives and
the operator made corrections based on an expert cost function. Masone
et al. [18] proposed a shared framework for online planning of mobile
robots, where the operator was in charge of correcting the desired
path, the nominal behavior was generated by the artificial potential
field, and the blending filter fused the nominal behavior and operator’s
inputs smoothly. Corredor [19] proposed an assistance method for
teleoperation, where the drift-diffusion model was built as the decision-
maker to determine the assistance levels. Kim et al. [20] proposed a
shared control strategy for single-cell microinjection tasks, where the
nominal behavior was generated by the artificial potential field and
the Fitts’ law as well as the steering law were adopted to quantify
and determine the optimal blending parameters. Estimating human
intention is a key and difficult issue for blending control strategies.
Estimation strategy design and human perception of the interaction
tasks are two important factors in this process.
Haptic feedback helps operators make reasonable decisions and
behaviors. For example, the force feedback at the master side guides
the operator’s arm towards the position with the most comfort [21];
the haptic feedback guides the operator towards the position where the
estimation uncertainty is minimized [22]; the force feedback provided
to the operator is the function of the error between the desired and ac-
tual path tracked by the slave robot [18]; and the haptic force feedback
provided to the operator is used to assist the operator in performing the
task [14]. The direct force-reflection (a type of haptic force feedback,
interaction force at the slave side is proportionally transmitted to the
master side [23]) on operators facilitates their perception of the task
conducted at the slave side and allows for more accurate judgment and
corrective behavior in polishing tasks.
However, the direct force-reflection can cause contact instabili-
ties even without communication delays, such as violent recoiling at
the master side [23]. Some methods, such as the time domain pas-
sivity approach [24], wave-variables concept [25], and energy tank
approach [26], are proposed to solve the problem, which are widely
used in bilateral teleoperation. Panzirsch et al. [27] presented a tele-
operation approach with force feedback at extreme delays based on
the time domain passivity approach, and the method was validated in
the Analog-1 experiment. Heck et al. [23] proposed a two-layer ap-
proach for bilateral teleoperation with direct force-reflection and time
delays, where the passivity layer guaranteed the system’s passivity.
Lee et al. [28] proposed a teleoperation system with multiple safety
constraints based on quadratic programming, where the energy tank
method was adopted to preserve the system’s passivity. Although direct
force-reflection helps operators perceive interaction force at the slave
side, there is little research on introducing direct force-reflection into
corrective shared control due to the instability it causes.
To sum up, proper estimation of human intention is a key technol-
ogy for shared-control-based HRC. The accuracy of this estimation is
influenced by the intention estimation strategy and human perception
of the interaction tasks. direct force-reflection facilitates estimating the
human intention, but it will lead to contact instability in corrective
shared control.
To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first research on
incorporating direct force-reflection in corrective shared control, and
the contributions of this paper are twofold.
(1) A corrective shared control architecture with direct force-
reflection is proposed to allow operators to deal with unexpected
issues in polishing tasks, under which an intention-based collab-
orative polishing strategy is proposed to correct the polishing
force.
(2) Based on the energy-awareness theory, a coupling energy scaling
policy is designed between master and slave sides to regulate
the transmission of both energy flows, which guarantees the
passivity of the whole system and avoids contact instability
caused by direct force-reflection.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces
the human–robot collaborative polishing strategy. Section 3 presents
the shared control architecture with direct force-reflection. Section 4
validates the proposed strategy and architecture through experimental
studies. Section 5 concludes this paper.
2. Human–robot collaborative polishing strategy
In this section, we propose a human–robot collaborative polishing
strategy for correcting polishing force based on human intention, as
shown in Fig. 1.
2.1. Problem formulation
Human intention can directly determine the degree of correct-
ing polishing force from operators, where estimating human intention
is a critical technology for corrective shared control. Proper estima-
tion of human intention facilitates better handling of emergencies and
unexpected issues in polishing tasks.
Human intention consists of direct and indirect human intention
[29]. Direct human intention represents the instant or natural re-
sponse when an abnormality occurs [30]. Indirect human intention
implies the goal of achieving a task. For example, the slave robot is
expected to polish workpieces with constant force when unexpected
issues disappear. For the human–robot collaborative polishing strategy,
the nominal robot behavior generated by a force-tracking controller can
satisfy the indirect human intention. Besides, operators perceive the
performance of the polishing task through visual and haptic feedback.
The direct human intention is generated to correct the polishing force at
the slave side when unexpected issues occur. The correcting polishing
force increases with the strength of the direct human intention.
The challenges associated with the human–robot collaborative pol-
ishing strategy are (i) selecting a suitable natural response, such as
velocity and force, representing human direct intention; and (ii) de-
signing a plausible human intention estimator according to the natural
response.

3. Shared control architecture with direct force-reflection
In this section, we propose a shared control architecture consisting
of two layers to enhance human perception of polishing tasks as shown
in Fig. 2. The human–robot collaborative polishing strategy and direct
force-reflection are implemented in the transparency layer. The energy
scaling is introduced in the passivity layer to deal with the non-
passivity caused by the asymmetry in the transparency layer. Energy
flow is fundamental in the proposed architecture, and the energy
analysis is shown in Fig. 3. Energy flow in the proposed architecture is
embodied in three aspects: (i) energy-behavior conversion, for example,
the energy in the slave tank supports the polishing force between
the slave robot and workpieces; (ii) dissipated power produced by
the robots is injected into the corresponding tank; and (iii) energy
regulation based on the coupling energy scaling policy between the
master and slave tanks guarantees the system’s passivity.

4. Experimental studies
In the section, two experiments are conducted in polishing tasks
to verify our proposal sufficiently: (i) polishing effect, polishing two
workpieces in Fig. 4 with four methods to compare and analyze our
proposal with other methods; and (ii) user experience, 17 subjects are
invited to polish two workpieces and ours is compared with the latest
shared control strategy using 14 subjective metrics.
As shown in Fig. 4 , a polishing experimental setup is built to
verify the proposed corrective shared control architecture with di-
rect force-reflection through a shared-control-based HRC system. The
shared-control-based HRC system consists of a master robot (omega
7) with a maximum feedback force of 8 N, a slave robot (ROKAE
Xmate7Pro) with a maximum load of 7 kg, and a force/torque sensor
(Kunwei KWR75) with a maximum force of 200 N and a maximum
torque of 8 N m. A polishing tool with a speed of 200 RPM is equipped
to the endpoint of the slave robot. The polishing tool sticking an 800-
mesh sandpaper is used to polish the workpieces in Fig. 4 (c) roughly.
The polishing task includes the following two experimental conditions
with different materials, shapes, and polishing surfaces: (i) polishing
a flat steel plate with the size of 400 mm × 400 mm in Fig. 4 (c1),
and (ii) polishing a slope aluminum extrusion with an angle of 8 ◦ in
Fig. 4 (c2). The polished workpieces have three areas: normal area-1

is painted in yellow, and the polishing sequence is ‘NA1-HPA-NA2’.
The master robot, which is operated by users, is used to correct the
polishing force while polishing the HPA. The communication between
the master and slave robots and sample frequencies are all set at 1 kHz.

4.3. Discussion
In this section, two experiments are conducted to validate our
proposal in the two experimental conditions as shown in Fig. 4. In
the first experiment, the results of 8 groups of contrastive exper-
iments (the proposed human–robot collaborative polishing strategy
versus manual polishing, automatic polishing, and the latest shared
control strategy in the two experimental conditions) indicate that (i)
the proposed human–robot collaborative polishing strategy combines
the advantages of both manual polishing and autonomous polishing
like the latest shared control strategy; (ii) the proposed corrective
shared control architecture effectively provides the operator with direct
force-reflection while guaranteeing the stability of the human–robot
collaborative polishing system. In the second experiment, 17 subjects
are invited to polish the workpiece with four correction approaches in
the two experimental conditions. The results indicate that the direct
force-reflection enhances the accuracy of estimating human intention to

The proposed corrective shared architecture has the potential to be
applied in industrial scenarios although only the 400 mm × 400 mm
steel plate and slope aluminum extrusion are polished in the experimen-
tal setup in Fig. 4. The polished workpieces involve different materials,
shape and polishing surfaces. Considering the large size variation of
polished workpieces in industrial scenarios, we further provided two
solutions for workpieces in different sizes. For small-sized workpieces,
a suitable polishing tool in Fig. 4 is required to be replaced and the
scaling matrices 𝑩𝑠𝑚 and 𝑩𝑠𝑎 in (4) should be reduced. Besides, a
camera with a long focal length is required to allow the operator to
scrutinize the polished workpiece if the workpiece is small in size. For
large-sized workpieces, only the scaling matrices 𝑩𝑠𝑚 and 𝑩𝑠𝑎 in (4) are
required to be adjusted if the workpiece is within the workspace of the
slave manipulator (the parameter can be referred to the manipulator
manual); otherwise the large workpiece can be placed on a conveyor
belt, the slave manipulator can be equipped to a mobile base such as a
mobile robot [38] or rail [39], or the slave manipulator can be replaced
with long-stroke manipulators [40] (we recommend the first scheme
because the kinematics of the manipulators are more complex in the
latter two schemes).
The most important components in the proposed architecture are
the master and slave sides. The proposed architecture is extremely
robust and protective for operators on the master side. The architecture
prevents operators from being affected by the field environment, such
as noise, because operators can correct the polishing force in a comfort-
able and safe area. Only one parameter ̇ 𝑥𝑚𝑎𝑥
𝑚 denoting the maximum
correction velocity in (5) is required to be calibrated in advance, and
the same parameter is generally suitable for different operators because
human arms move at the same speed essentially. For the slave side,
the sandpaper sticking to the polishing tool in Fig. 4 is required to be
replaced regularly as with other methods [3,7,8]. A camera is required
to allow operators to observe the polished workpieces, and the camera
must be protected from dust.
The proposed architecture avoids direct and physical interaction
between the operator and polishing tool, as shown in Fig. 4, and
the corrective behaviors are transmitted from the operator to the
slave side through communication. Therefore, communication is an
important part of the proposed architecture, and communication that
may affect polishing tasks consists of the following three aspects: (i)
communication quality is mainly represented by packet loss rate that
indicates the ratio of the number of lost packets to the number of
sent packets [41]. A low packet loss rate is necessary for polishing
tasks, which guarantees that the corrective behaviors from the operator
can be completely transmitted to the slave side; (ii) communication
frequency determines the density of corrective behaviors transmitted to
the slave side. A higher communication frequency allows a more perfect
reflection of the operator’s corrective behaviors in polishing tasks; and
(iii) communication delay can result in the instability of the system
and usually occurs when the master and slave sides are far apart [27].
In summary, low communication quality will result in system failure.
It is fortunate that communication technology is very sophisticated,

5. Conclusion and future work
This paper proposes a corrective shared control architecture with
direct force-reflection for polishing tasks with unexpected issues. In par-
ticular, an intention-based human–robot collaborative polishing strat-
egy is proposed under the proposed architecture. Direct force-reflection
is introduced in corrective shared control for the first time. The pro-
posed architecture guarantees operators’ safety [8], which is reflected
in the following two aspects: (i) the proposed architecture belonging
to shared-control-based HRC avoids the direct and physical interaction
between the operator and workpiece in comparison with impedance-
control-based HRC; and (ii) direct force-reflection will result in contact
instability although it facilitates operators’ perception of polishing tasks
and the contact instability will result in oscillatory behaviors of the
master and slave robots that will cause damage to the polishing tool
and polished workpieces. A coupling energy scaling policy between the
master and slave sides is designed to address the issue, which is verified
theoretically and experimentally.
Polishing experiments are conducted to verify the proposed archi-
tecture based on the polishing effect and user experience. Eight groups
of contrastive experiments indicate that the proposed human–robot
collaborative polishing strategy is compatible with the advantages of
both manual polishing and autonomous polishing like the latest shared
control strategy, and the proposed corrective shared control architec-
ture effectively provides the operator with direct force-reflection while
guaranteeing the stability of the human–robot collaborative polishing
system. Seventeen subjects think that the human–robot collaborative
polishing strategy (with respect to the latest shared control strategy)
implemented in the corrective shared control architecture with direct
force-reflection gives them the best user experience and the direct
force-reflection enhances their perception of the polishing force at the
slave side.
Currently, the proposed architecture is implemented in an experi-
mental polishing task where the flat steel plate and slope aluminum
extrusion are adopted. Workpieces with complex geometries are not
considered in the paper because we mainly focus on correcting the
polishing force. Future work will focus on implementing the proposed
architecture in industrial polishing tasks and considering the safety
of teleoperation systems in the presence of large time delays. The
complexity of workpieces in industrial polishing tasks is reflected in
the following two aspects: geometry complexity and size complexity.
Workpieces of various sizes will also be considered, which requires the
aid of a telephoto camera (for small-sized workpieces) and a conveyor
belt (for large-sized workpieces). Besides, the instability caused by
the communication delay between the master and slave sides will be
addressed based on the assumption that the operator and the polished
workpiece will be far apart.
